5.2 Distributed Systems Fundamentals

Objective: understand replication, sharding, consensus, and the CAP theorem deeply enough to reason about failure scenarios, not just define the terms - extends 4.8's HLD fundamentals into failure-mode detail.

5.2a Replication & Consensus

Leader-based (single-leader) replication: one node (the leader) accepts all writes and propagates them to followers; reads can be served from the leader (always fresh) or followers (possibly slightly stale, spreads read load). Leaderless replication: no single leader - clients write to multiple replicas directly (a "quorum"), and reads query multiple replicas too, reconciling differences (via version numbers/timestamps) at read time.

Consensus is how distributed nodes agree on a single, consistent view of "what actually happened" (e.g. who's the current leader, or the order operations occurred in) despite failures - algorithms like Raft and Paxos solve this formally.

Plain English: leader-based is simpler to reason about (one source of truth for writes), but the leader is a natural bottleneck/single point of failure for writes - if it goes down, a new leader must be elected, which is itself a consensus problem. Leaderless avoids that single point of failure for writes (any replica accepts writes) but pushes complexity onto reconciling conflicting versions, since there's no single authoritative order of events.

Analogy: leader-based is one teacher (leader) grading all papers, the sole source of "the official grade," with TAs (followers) each keeping a copy for students to check without bothering the teacher - fast reads, but if the teacher's unavailable, official new grading stops until a substitute (new leader) is agreed on. Leaderless is several equally-authorized graders any student can submit to - no single point of failure, but if two graders scored the same paper differently (a genuine version conflict), someone reconciles which is correct after the fact.

class LeaderReplicationSystem:              # illustrative, not literally distributed
    def __init__(self):
        self.leader = "node_A"
        self.followers = ["node_B", "node_C"]
    def write(self, data):
        self._write_to(self.leader, data)
        for follower in self.followers:
            self._replicate_to(follower, data)

Raft/Paxos, conceptually: nodes vote to elect a leader (a term-based election requiring a majority/quorum), and once elected, the leader's proposed order of operations is what everyone follows - until it fails and a new election happens. This is what lets a cluster of unreliable, individually failure-prone machines behave, from the outside, like one single reliable source of truth.

5.2b Sharding & Hotspots

A hotspot occurs when a sharding scheme routes a disproportionate share of traffic/data to one shard, because the chosen shard key doesn't distribute load evenly - even if it distributes total data volume evenly, it can still create a hotspot if it doesn't also distribute access frequency evenly.

Plain English: a genuinely common real mistake - picking a shard key that looks reasonable ("shard by user_id, evenly hashed") but if a small number of "celebrity" users generate a wildly disproportionate share of all traffic, their shard becomes overloaded regardless of how evenly everyone else's data hashed. The scheme balanced data volume/count correctly but not access frequency, which is what actually determines load.

Analogy: splitting customer support tickets evenly by customer name alphabetically (A–M to team 1, N–Z to team 2) - perfectly balanced by customer count, but if 90% of actual ticket volume comes from 3 giant enterprise customers whose names happen to fall in A–M, team 1 drowns while team 2 is nearly idle. The split was balanced by the wrong metric.

Concrete scenario: a ride-sharing app sharding trip records by pickup city - most cities generate modest, comparable volume, but the "New York City" shard receives 50x the traffic of any other, since NYC alone generates far more rides than most other cities combined. The shard key (city) seemed like a natural, semantically meaningful partition, but directly encoded a massive real-world activity skew.

Mitigations: salting - adding a random or hash-based suffix to spread one hot key's data across multiple shards artificially (at the cost of needing to query multiple shards to reassemble that entity's full data); or choosing a genuinely higher-cardinality, more evenly-accessed key (e.g. shard by trip_id hash rather than city, accepting that city-scoped queries now fan out across all shards instead of hitting one).
