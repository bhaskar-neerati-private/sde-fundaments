5.2 Distributed Systems Fundamentals

Objective: understand replication, sharding, consensus, and the CAP theorem deeply enough to reason about failure scenarios, not just define the terms - extends 4.8's HLD fundamentals into failure-mode detail.

5.2a Replication & Consensus

Leader-based (single-leader) replication: one node (the leader) accepts all writes and propagates them to followers; reads can be served from the leader (always fresh) or followers (possibly slightly stale, spreads read load). Leaderless replication: no single leader - clients write to multiple replicas directly (a "quorum"), and reads query multiple replicas too, reconciling differences (via version numbers/timestamps) at read time.

Consensus is how distributed nodes agree on a single, consistent view of "what actually happened" (e.g. who's the current leader, or the order operations occurred in) despite failures - algorithms like Raft and Paxos solve this formally.

Plain English: leader-based is simpler to reason about (one source of truth for writes), but the leader is a natural bottleneck/single point of failure for writes - if it goes down, a new leader must be elected, which is itself a consensus problem. Leaderless avoids that single point of failure for writes (any replica accepts writes) but pushes complexity onto reconciling conflicting versions, since there's no single authoritative order of events.

Analogy: leader-based is one teacher (leader) grading all papers, the sole source of "the official grade," with TAs (followers) each keeping a copy for students to check without bothering the teacher - fast reads, but if the teacher's unavailable, official new grading stops until a substitute (new leader) is agreed on. Leaderless is several equally-authorized graders any student can submit to - no single point of failure, but if two graders scored the same paper differently (a genuine version conflict), someone reconciles which is correct after the fact.

class LeaderReplicationSystem:              # illustrative, not literally distributed
    def __init__(self):
        self.leader = "node_A"
        self.followers = ["node_B", "node_C"]
    def write(self, data):
        self._write_to(self.leader, data)
        for follower in self.followers:
            self._replicate_to(follower, data)

Raft/Paxos, conceptually: nodes vote to elect a leader (a term-based election requiring a majority/quorum), and once elected, the leader's proposed order of operations is what everyone follows - until it fails and a new election happens. This is what lets a cluster of unreliable, individually failure-prone machines behave, from the outside, like one single reliable source of truth.

Why electing a new leader is itself "a consensus problem," not a simple mechanical handoff: every remaining node needs to agree on which replacement node becomes the new leader - if two nodes each believed themselves elected simultaneously (a "split brain"), the system would have two conflicting sources of truth accepting writes independently, exactly the failure leader-based replication was supposed to prevent. Requiring a majority/quorum vote (more than half of all nodes) guarantees at most one candidate can ever achieve that majority at a given time, which is precisely what prevents two simultaneous leaders from both being legitimately elected.

Why leaderless replication's reconciliation is fundamentally harder than it sounds: without a single leader dictating write order, two replicas can each independently accept a different write to the same key at nearly the same time, with no inherent way to know which "should" win - version numbers/timestamps help detect that a conflict occurred, but resolving which value is correct (or how to merge them) often requires either accepting one arbitrarily (last-write-wins, which can silently discard a legitimate concurrent write) or application-specific merge logic. This is a genuinely different and harder problem than leader-based replication's simpler "the leader's order is authoritative" model.

5.2b Sharding & Hotspots

A hotspot occurs when a sharding scheme routes a disproportionate share of traffic/data to one shard, because the chosen shard key doesn't distribute load evenly - even if it distributes total data volume evenly, it can still create a hotspot if it doesn't also distribute access frequency evenly.

Plain English: a genuinely common real mistake - picking a shard key that looks reasonable ("shard by user_id, evenly hashed") but if a small number of "celebrity" users generate a wildly disproportionate share of all traffic, their shard becomes overloaded regardless of how evenly everyone else's data hashed. The scheme balanced data volume/count correctly but not access frequency, which is what actually determines load.

Analogy: splitting customer support tickets evenly by customer name alphabetically (A-M to team 1, N-Z to team 2) - perfectly balanced by customer count, but if 90% of actual ticket volume comes from 3 giant enterprise customers whose names happen to fall in A-M, team 1 drowns while team 2 is nearly idle. The split was balanced by the wrong metric.

Concrete scenario: a ride-sharing app sharding trip records by pickup city - most cities generate modest, comparable volume, but the "New York City" shard receives 50x the traffic of any other, since NYC alone generates far more rides than most other cities combined. The shard key (city) seemed like a natural, semantically meaningful partition, but directly encoded a massive real-world activity skew.

Mitigations: salting - adding a random or hash-based suffix to spread one hot key's data across multiple shards artificially (at the cost of needing to query multiple shards to reassemble that entity's full data); or choosing a genuinely higher-cardinality, more evenly-accessed key (e.g. shard by trip_id hash rather than city, accepting that city-scoped queries now fan out across all shards instead of hitting one).

Why salting introduces a real query-cost trade-off, not a free fix: spreading one hot key's data across multiple physical shards means a query that used to hit exactly one shard (e.g. "all of NYC's trips") now has to fan out to every shard the data was salted across and merge the results - the hotspot problem is solved at the cost of turning what was a simple, single-shard query into a more expensive scatter-gather across multiple shards. This is a genuine trade-off, not a strictly-better fix: it's worth applying specifically when the hotspot's cost (one overloaded shard degrading for everyone) outweighs the added query complexity, not automatically.

Why "distributes data volume evenly" and "distributes access frequency evenly" are genuinely different properties, worth holding apart explicitly: a shard key can satisfy one without the other, as the celebrity-user and NYC examples both show - the number of rows assigned to a shard says nothing about how often those specific rows get read or written. Evaluating a candidate shard key requires checking both properties separately; checking only data volume (the easier one to measure statically, without needing real traffic patterns) is precisely how hotspot bugs slip through design review undetected until production load reveals them.

Common mistakes & misconceptions

  1. Choosing a shard key based only on even data-volume distribution, without separately checking whether access frequency is also evenly distributed - as emphasized above, these are genuinely independent properties, and a key satisfying only the first can still produce a severe hotspot.
  2. Believing leaderless replication has "no single point of failure" and therefore no real failure modes to worry about. It trades the leader's single-point-of-failure risk for a different, genuinely hard problem: reconciling conflicting concurrent writes with no authoritative ordering - not a strictly safer design, just a differently-shaped one.
  3. Assuming a new leader election is a simple, instantaneous handoff. As explained above, it's a consensus problem in its own right, requiring a majority/quorum agreement specifically to prevent a split-brain scenario where two nodes simultaneously believe themselves to be the legitimate leader.
  4. Applying salting to every shard key "just in case," without weighing the added scatter-gather query cost against an actual, verified hotspot risk. Salting is a targeted mitigation for a specific, identified hotspot problem, not a default best practice to apply universally regardless of whether the access pattern actually warrants it.
  5. Reading from a follower and assuming the data is exactly as fresh as the leader's. Leader-based replication's follower reads are explicitly called out as "possibly slightly stale" - an application that requires strict read-your-own-writes consistency needs to either read from the leader for that specific case, or use a replication scheme that provides that guarantee explicitly, rather than assuming follower reads are interchangeable with leader reads.
